How this works
  1. Doves don't leave on a schedule — they leave when a cold front pushes through and gives them a north wind to ride.
  2. So we watch four stretches of country north of your fields: Oklahoma, southern Kansas, northern Kansas, and Nebraska. They stop at the edges of the Central Flyway — birds further east than that go down the Mississippi, not to Texas.
  3. When a front crosses one of them, the birds staged there take off with it. They ride the north wind while it holds — up to about 200 miles in a day — and sit down when it quits. We walk them south one day at a time on the wind they actually get, so a stalling front means birds that stall with it.
  4. The front moves faster than the birds do. So the cool weather gets to you first, and the birds show up a day or two behind it. That gap is what this whole thing is for.

The front, then the birds

The cold front gets here first — you'll feel the wind switch and the temperature drop. The birds it kicked loose are still in the air. They land a day or two later. Don't confuse the two.

Day by day at your fields

What it's actually doing where you'll be standing. North wind is shown in bold — that's post-front air, and it's what you want. Doves turn into the wind to land, so set up with it at your back and they work toward you instead of flaring off behind you.

Shooting hours shown are Texas dove — a half hour before sunrise to sunset. Regulations change every year; confirm against the current TPWD Outdoor Annual before you rely on it.

Are the birds actually here?

Doves counted per hour by birdwatchers in the area — adjusted for how many people were out looking, so it counts birds and not birders. Collared-doves are the check: they don't migrate, so their line should stay flat when the others jump. If all three move together, we're just watching birdwatchers.

How much to trust this

The weather half has been checked against eleven years of records. The bird half hasn't been checked at all yet.
