    How the duck forecast works
    1. Ducks leave when their water up north freezes or snows over — we track that with the weather severity index ducks are known to answer to (Schummer et al. 2010).
    2. They fly at night, leaving about 45 minutes after sunset, and only when the wind ~1,500 ft up is behind them, it's dry, and it isn't socked in.
    3. One night carries them 300–500+ miles — farther the harder the cold came.
    4. So the freeze line moving south is the thing to watch. New birds land at dawn, a night or two behind it.

    How this works
    1. Doves don't leave on a schedule — they go when a cold front gives them a north wind to ride.
    2. So we watch four stretches of country upstream of you, out to 620 miles north.
    3. Birds ride that wind while it holds — up to about 200 miles in a day — and sit down when it quits.
    4. The front outruns the birds. Cool air reaches you first; they land a day or two behind it. That gap is the whole point.

    How much to trust this

    The weather half has been checked against eleven years of records. The bird half is now graded every morning against what eBird birders counted — that scorecard is below, and it is the rule for publishing a flyway.

    Checked and working

    • The weather severity index reproduces the published worked example exactly (Schummer et al. 2010)
    • Wind is read at a real height above the ground — never from a level that is underground on the High Plains
    • Snow depth matches an independent source to the centimetre

    Still guesswork

    • Every threshold comes from studies in the Mississippi and Atlantic flyways — none was fit here
    • How many ducks are up north — the model only knows relative numbers
    • Teal and puddle-duck triggers are rougher than the mallard one
    • Whether it has predicted a single duck — duck counting only began Oct 4

    Checked and working

    • Front speeds — — typical across — fronts, 2015–2025
    • About 7 big continent-wide fronts a season, which matches a real dove season
    • Same answer on any machine — no randomness hiding in it

    Still guesswork

    • How far a dove really covers on a given tailwind — that number sets the arrival day
    • How fast the birds up north run out over a season
    • Strength labels aren't calibrated — read them as bigger-vs-smaller, not as counts
    • White-winged doves — the forecast counts them the same as mourning doves, but how far and how fast they travel on a front comes from mourning-dove studies; white-wing movement is barely studied. The scorecard grades against both together, so a gap would show up there
    • Whether any of this has ever correctly predicted a single bird — one promising day so far (Collin County, Sep 22)

    The scorecard

    Each forecast is checked 1–3 days later against dove (mourning and white-winged together)duck counts at the nearest eBird circle, with collared doves (which don't migrate) as the check on birdwatcher effort. It has to beat the calendar, not just chance. A flyway is published only after it passes on at least three weeks of record — and the Central flyway is held to that same bar.
