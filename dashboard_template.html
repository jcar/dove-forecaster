Simulated weather. Real wind history does not exist yet, so this page is running on an invented field — plausible fronts marching south at real-world speeds. Where do you hunt?

Type a town in Texas or Oklahoma. You get the forecast for that ground — its own cold fronts, its own arrival days — not a guess borrowed from somewhere nearby.

    The whole system, moving

    Cold fronts sweep south down the plains. Birds leave when one reaches them, ride the north wind while it holds, and sit down when it quits. Press play and watch it happen.

    What you're watching

    The front arrives first. Watch the bright edge sweep down, then the warm dots bloom behind it a day or two later. That gap is the whole reason this thing exists.

    Nothing is drawn between the dots. The flight model has no east-west position for a bird in the air, so we don't invent one. The front line joins places where a crossing was actually detected. Arrival sizes are relative, not counts.

    How this works
    1. Doves don't leave on a schedule — they go when a cold front gives them a north wind to ride.
    2. So we watch four stretches of country upstream of you, out to 620 miles north.
    3. Birds ride that wind while it holds — up to about 200 miles in a day — and sit down when it quits.
    4. The front outruns the birds. Cool air reaches you first; they land a day or two behind it. That gap is the whole point.

    Cold fronts we're tracking

    Each front is clocked as it crosses the bands, so its speed is measured rather than assumed. How sure comes from 31 separate weather model runs and how much they disagree.

    Are the birds actually moving?

    Real dove counts from Cornell's eBird, at seven latitudes from the Canadian line down to north Texas. Each row is compared only to its own last week — a quiet Nebraska county isn't judged against busy Dallas. Glowing means more doves than usual there; dark means fewer. A migration shows up as a glow that slides down the rows over days.
